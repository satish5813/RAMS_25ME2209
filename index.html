2. What is a robot?

An industrial robot is a reprogrammable, multifunctional manipulator designed to move material, parts, tools or devices through variable programmed motions to perform a variety of tasks. The key word is reprogrammable: unlike a cam-driven special-purpose machine (fixed automation), the same robot can be re-taught for a new part or task. This places robots in flexible automation.

A robot is a classic mechatronic system: a mechanical structure, actuators that move it, sensors that measure it, and a computer controller that decides what to do.

Joints. A revolute (R) joint allows rotation about an axis (joint variable θ); a prismatic (P) joint allows sliding along an axis (joint variable d). Each such joint gives one degree of freedom.

4. Degrees of freedom

For a serial (open-chain) arm, DOF = number of independent 1-DOF joints. To place a body anywhere in space with any orientation needs 6 DOF: 3 for position (x, y, z) and 3 for orientation (roll, pitch, yaw). An arm with more than 6 DOF is redundant (useful for avoiding obstacles); one with fewer cannot reach every pose.

For planar mechanisms (including closed chains) use the Grübler criterion:

F = 3(n − 1) − 2j1 − j2

where n is the number of links including the ground, j1 the number of 1-DOF joints and j2 the number of 2-DOF joints.

Worked example

Problem. (a) How many DOF does a 3R planar arm have? (b) A planar four-bar linkage has 4 links (including ground) and 4 revolute joints; find its DOF.

Solution. (a) Serial chain of three revolute joints: DOF = 3. Check with Grübler: n = 4 (ground + 3 links), j1 = 3, F = 3(4 − 1) − 2(3) = 9 − 6 = 3.

(b) n = 4, j1 = 4: F = 3(3) − 2(4) = 1. A four-bar is driven by a single input.

Key points to remember

  • Robots are reprogrammable, which makes them flexible automation.
  • Arm joints position the wrist; wrist joints orient the tool.
  • Six DOF are needed for an arbitrary position and orientation.
  • The configuration (first three joints) decides the shape of the workspace.

Recall before you start

Robot configurations and DOF from Session 1.

1. Performance specifications

TermMeaning
Workspace (work envelope)Set of all points the wrist or TCP can reach. The dexterous workspace is the part reachable in all orientations.
ReachMaximum distance from the base axis to the wrist centre.
PayloadMaximum load (including the end effector) carried at rated speed and accuracy.
ResolutionSmallest increment of motion the controller can command or detect.
AccuracyHow close the robot gets to a commanded point in space.
RepeatabilityHow close the robot returns to a previously taught point (e.g. ±0.02 mm). Usually much better than accuracy.

Taught programs (teach pendant) depend on repeatability; programs computed offline from CAD depend on accuracy.

2. End effectors

  • Mechanical grippers — two- or three-finger, parallel or angular jaws; hold parts by friction or by enclosing them.
  • Vacuum grippers — suction cups for flat, smooth parts (sheets, cartons, glass).
  • Magnetic grippers — electromagnets for ferrous parts.
  • Adaptive / soft grippers — compliant fingers for irregular or delicate objects.
  • Tools — welding torch, spot-weld gun, spray gun, screwdriver, deburring spindle.
  • Compliance devices (e.g. remote centre compliance) help insert pegs into holes despite small misalignment.

For a friction gripper with n contact surfaces and friction coefficient μ, the gripping force F must satisfy:

n μ F ≥ m g × SF

where SF is a safety factor (typically 2–3) that covers accelerations during motion.

3. The robot as a mechatronic system

Every robot repeats a sense → decide → act loop. Sensors (encoders, vision, force) measure the robot and its surroundings; the controller (computer, motion cards, PLC) compares the measurement with the plan and computes commands; drives and actuators (servo motors with gearboxes, pneumatics) move the links.

BlockExamples
SensingJoint encoders, limit switches, cameras, force/torque sensors

4. Industrial applications

  • Material handling: palletizing, machine tending, pick-and-place.
  • Processing: arc and spot welding, painting, grinding, deburring.
  • Assembly and inspection: screwing, insertion, vision inspection.
  • Logistics and services: warehouse picking, AGVs, medical and collaborative tasks.

Worked example

Problem 1. A 2R planar arm has l1 = 0.4 m and l2 = 0.3 m with full 360° joint ranges. Describe its workspace and find its area.

Solution. The tip can be anywhere between radius |l1 − l2| = 0.1 m and l1 + l2 = 0.7 m: an annulus. Area = π(0.7² − 0.1²) = π(0.48) ≈ 1.51 m².

Problem 2. A two-finger friction gripper (μ = 0.3) must hold a 2 kg part with SF = 2. Find the minimum gripping force.

Solution. 2 × 0.3 × F ≥ 2 × 9.81 × 2 gives F ≥ 39.24 / 0.6 = 65.4 N.

Key points to remember

  • Repeatability matters for taught programs; accuracy matters for offline programs.
  • Payload includes the weight of the gripper itself.
  • Gripping force is sized for the worst-case acceleration, not just the weight.
  • A robot is a sense–decide–act mechatronic system.

Recall before you start

Matrix multiplication, transpose, determinant; sine and cosine of standard angles.

1. Frames, position and orientation

A frame is a set of three mutually perpendicular unit vectors attached at an origin. We use a fixed base frame {0} (or {A}) and frames attached to each link and to the tool. A point P is described in frame {A} by its position vector Ap = [px py pz]T.

The orientation of frame {B} relative to {A} is described by the 3×3 rotation matrix ARB, whose columns are the unit vectors xB, yB, zB expressed in {A}. If a point is known in {B}, then:

Ap = ARB Bp (pure rotation, common origin)

3. Properties

  • Columns (and rows) are orthonormal unit vectors.
  • R−1 = RT, so BRA = (ARB)T.
  • det R = +1 (a proper rotation; −1 would be a reflection).
  • Rotations do not commute: RxRz ≠ RzRx in general.

4. Composing rotations

  • Rotation about the current (moving) axes: post-multiply. R = R1R2.
  • Rotation about the fixed axes: pre-multiply. R = R2R1.

Three-angle descriptions: Z-Y-Z Euler angles (R = Rz(φ)Ry(θ)Rz(ψ)) and roll-pitch-yaw (R = Rz(φ)Ry(θ)Rx(ψ), about fixed axes x, y, z in that order). Robot controllers display tool orientation in one of these forms.

0Tn = A1(q1) A2(q2) … An(qn)

The upper-left 3×3 block of 0Tn is the tool orientation; the last column is the tool position. FK always has a unique answer.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why inverse kinematics (IK) can have zero, one, several or infinite solutions.
  • Solve the IK of a 2R planar arm by algebraic and geometric methods, including both elbow solutions.
  • Extend the method to a 3R planar arm with a specified tool orientation and to SCARA.

Recall before you start

FK of the 2R arm (Session 6), atan2, law of cosines.

1. The IK problem

IK asks: given the desired tool pose, what joint values achieve it? The equations are nonlinear, so: a target outside the workspace has no solution; a target inside usually has more than one (elbow-up/elbow-down); a redundant arm has infinitely many. Always use the two-argument atan2(y, x), which returns the angle in the correct quadrant.

2. 2R arm — algebraic solution

Square and add the FK equations to eliminate θ1:

D = cos θ2 = (x² + y² − l1² − l2²) / (2 l1 l2)
θ2 = atan2(±√(1 − D²), D)
θ1 = atan2(y, x) − atan2(l2 sin θ2, l1 + l2 cos θ2)

The target is reachable only if |D| ≤ 1. The + sign gives one elbow configuration and the − sign the other.

3. Geometric view

Join the base to the target: this line (length r = √(x²+y²), angle β = atan2(y, x)) and the two links form a triangle. The law of cosines gives the elbow angle; the angle ψ between the line and link 1 gives θ1 = β ∓ ψ, where cos ψ = (r² + l1² − l2²)/(2 l1 r). The geometric method gives insight; the algebraic one is easier to program.

4. 3R planar arm and SCARA

  • 3R with orientation φ: find the wrist point (xw, yw) = (x − l3cos φ, y − l3sin φ), solve the 2R IK for θ1, θ2, then θ3 = φ − θ1 − θ2.
  • SCARA: θ1, θ2 from the 2R solution on (x, y); d3 from z; θ4 from the required tool rotation.

Worked example

Problem. For l1 = 0.5 m, l2 = 0.3 m, find all joint solutions that place the tool at (0.511, 0.540) m (the FK answer of Session 6).

Use the unrounded target x = 0.5106 m, y = 0.5398 m: x² + y² = 0.2607 + 0.2914 = 0.5521. D = (0.5521 − 0.25 − 0.09)/(2 × 0.5 × 0.3) = 0.2121/0.3 = 0.707.

θ2 = ±45°. atan2(y, x) = atan2(0.540, 0.511) = 46.6°.

Solution A (θ2 = +45°): atan2(0.3 × 0.707, 0.5 + 0.3 × 0.707) = atan2(0.212, 0.712) = 16.6°, so θ1 = 46.6 − 16.6 = 30.0° (matches Session 6).

Solution B (θ2 = −45°): θ1 = 46.6 + 16.6 = 63.2°. Check with FK: both place the tool at the same point.

Key points to remember

  • IK may have no, one, several or infinite solutions.
  • 2R IK: θ2 from the law of cosines, then θ1 with atan2.
  • Reachability test: |D| ≤ 1.
  • Decouple: wrist position first, then orientation.

Recall before you start

FK of the 2R arm; differentiation; 2×2 matrix inverse.

1. Velocity kinematics

Differentiating FK with respect to time gives a linear relation between joint rates q′ (dq/dt) and tool velocity v:

v = J(q) q′

J is the Jacobian: its columns show how each joint moves the tool. For a 6-DOF arm, J is 6×6 (3 rows of linear, 3 of angular velocity).

2. Jacobian of the 2R arm

det J = l1 l2 sin θ2

3. Singularities

Where det J = 0, J cannot be inverted: the tool loses the ability to move in some direction, and the joint rates needed for a nearby motion become very large. For the 2R arm this happens at θ2 = 0° (arm stretched, boundary of workspace) and θ2 = 180° (arm folded). Yoshikawa's manipulability measure w = √det(J JT) (= |det J| for a square J) indicates how far a pose is from singularity; for the 2R arm it is largest at θ2 = ±90°.

By the principle of virtual work, joint torques that balance a tool force F are:

τ = JT F

Applications: resolved-rate (Cartesian velocity) control, force estimation, avoiding singular paths in welding and painting.

Learning objectives

  • Choose generalized coordinates for a robotic system.
  • Write the kinetic and potential energy of rotating and translating links.
  • Apply the Lagrange equation to obtain the equation of motion of a single-link arm.

Recall before you start

Mass moment of inertia (rod about its end: ml²/3; about its centre: ml²/12), Newton's laws. Notation: θ′ = dθ/dt (joint velocity), θ″ = d²θ/dt² (joint acceleration).

1. Why dynamics?

Kinematics tells us where the robot moves; dynamics tells us what torques the actuators must supply to move it that way. Dynamic models are used to size motors and gearboxes, to simulate robots and in advanced (model-based) controllers.

2. Generalized coordinates

A set of independent variables that completely fixes the configuration. For a serial arm, the joint variables q = [q1 … qn]T (angles θi or displacements di) are the natural choice; their number equals the DOF. The matching generalized forces are joint torques (revolute) or forces (prismatic).

3. Energy of a link

K = ½ m vc² + ½ Ic ω² P = m g hc

vc is the velocity of the centre of mass, Ic the moment of inertia about the centre of mass, ω the angular velocity and hc the height of the centre of mass. For a link rotating about a fixed pivot you may use K = ½ IO ω² with IO about the pivot. The robot's total K and P are sums over all links.

4. Lagrange's equation

L = K − P, τi = d/dt (∂L/∂qi′) − ∂L/∂qi

The procedure: (1) choose q; (2) write K and P in terms of q and q′; (3) form L; (4) differentiate. The result always has the structure inertia × acceleration + velocity terms + gravity terms = joint torque.

Worked example

Problem. A uniform link (mass m = 2 kg, length l = 0.5 m) rotates in a vertical plane about a pivot at one end; θ is measured from the horizontal. Find the equation of motion and the torque needed for θ″ = 2 rad/s² at θ = 0.

K = ½ (ml²/3) θ′²; P = m g (l/2) sin θ. L = K − P.

∂L/∂θ′ = (ml²/3) θ′, so d/dt(∂L/∂θ′) = (ml²/3) θ″; ∂L/∂θ = −m g (l/2) cos θ.

τ = (ml²/3) θ″ + m g (l/2) cos θ

Numbers: ml²/3 = 2 × 0.25 / 3 = 0.1667 kg·m². τ = 0.1667 × 2 + 2 × 9.81 × 0.25 × 1 = 0.333 + 4.905 = 5.24 N·m. Note how gravity dominates.

Key points to remember

  • Generalized coordinates for a serial arm = joint variables.
  • K = ½mv² + ½Iω²; P = mgh of the centre of mass.
  • τ = d/dt(∂L/∂q′) − ∂L/∂q.
  • Gravity terms often dominate the torque of a slow robot.

Recall before you start

Lagrangian method (Session 9). Notation: θ′ = dθ/dt (joint velocity), θ″ = d²θ/dt² (joint acceleration).

1. Newton–Euler in brief

  • Forward recursion (base → tip): compute the angular velocity, angular acceleration and linear acceleration of each link.
  • Backward recursion (tip → base): apply F = ma and the Euler equation N = Iα + ω × Iω to each link to get the force and moment each joint transmits; the component about the joint axis is the joint torque.

Newton–Euler gives the same result as Lagrange but is efficient for computers (cost grows linearly with n) and is used in robot controllers.

2. Two-link planar arm (point masses m1, m2 at the link ends)

τ1 = [(m1+m2)l1² + m2l2² + 2m2l1l2c2] θ1″ + [m2l2² + m2l1l2c2] θ2″
− m2l1l2s2 (2θ1′θ2′ + θ2′²) + (m1+m2) g l1c1 + m2 g l2c12

Angles are measured from the horizontal x axis. In matrix form: τ = M(q)q″ + C(q, q′)q′ + G(q).

3. From joint torque to motor torque

With a gearbox of ratio N and efficiency η, the motor must supply τm = τ / (N η) at speed ωm = N θ′. Size the motor for the worst case (peak acceleration at the pose of largest M and G).

Worked example

At rest all velocity and acceleration terms vanish; c1 = c12 = 1.

τ2 = m2 g l2 = 2 × 9.81 × 0.3 = 5.89 N·m.

τ1 = (m1+m2) g l1 + m2 g l2 = 4 × 9.81 × 0.5 + 5.89 = 19.62 + 5.89 = 25.51 N·m.

Motor torque for joint 1: 25.51 / (100 × 0.8) = 0.32 N·m.

Key points to remember

  • Newton–Euler: velocities forward, forces backward.
  • τ = M(q)q″ + C(q, q′)q′ + G(q).
  • Horizontal, stretched poses give the largest gravity torques.
  • Gearing divides torque by Nη and multiplies speed by N.

Recall before you start

Kinematic equations of motion with constant acceleration. Notation: θ′ = dθ/dt (joint velocity), θ″ = d²θ/dt² (joint acceleration).

1. Path versus trajectory

A path is the geometric route; a trajectory is a path with timing: position, velocity and acceleration as functions of time. Good trajectories are smooth (continuous velocity and acceleration), respect actuator limits and finish on time.

2. Joint space or Cartesian space?

3. PTP and CP motion

PTP: only start and goal matter (pick-and-place, spot welding). CP: the tool must follow the path accurately (arc welding, gluing, painting).

4. Trapezoidal (LSPB) velocity profile

Accelerate at constant a up to velocity V, cruise, then decelerate at −a. For a move of distance Δ:

tb = V / a, T = Δ/V + V/a (valid if Δ ≥ V²/a)

If Δ < V²/a, the profile becomes triangular and V is never reached. Velocity is continuous, but acceleration jumps at the blends, which causes jerk.

Worked example

Problem. A joint must rotate Δ = 90° (1.571 rad) with V = 1 rad/s and a = 2 rad/s². Plan an LSPB profile.

Check: V²/a = 0.5 rad < 1.571 rad, so a cruise segment exists.

tb = 1/2 = 0.5 s; T = 1.571/1 + 1/2 = 2.071 s.

Distance in the two blends = 2 × ½ × 2 × 0.5² = 0.5 rad; cruise distance = 1.071 rad, taking 1.071 s. Total 0.5 + 1.071 + 0.5 = 2.071 s ✓.

Key points to remember

  • Trajectory = path + timing.
  • Joint-space is simple and singularity-free; Cartesian-space gives a predictable tool path.
  • LSPB: tb = V/a, T = Δ/V + V/a.
  • Synchronise joints so that all finish at the same time.

Recall before you start

LSPB profile (Session 11). Notation: θ′ = dθ/dt (joint velocity), θ″ = d²θ/dt² (joint acceleration).

1. Cubic polynomial

θ(t) = a0 + a1t + a2t² + a3t³

Four conditions fix four coefficients: θ(0) = θ0, θ(T) = θf, θ′(0) = 0, θ′(T) = 0. Solving:

a0 = θ0, a1 = 0, a2 = 3(θf − θ0)/T², a3 = −2(θf − θ0)/T³

Peak velocity (at t = T/2) is 1.5Δ/T; acceleration is linear in time and largest at the ends (±6Δ/T²). The acceleration jumps from 0 to 6Δ/T² at start.

2. Quintic polynomial

Adding zero start and end accelerations needs six coefficients. For rest-to-rest motion with τ = t/T:

θ(t) = θ0 + Δ(10τ³ − 15τ⁴ + 6τ⁵)

Peak velocity 1.875Δ/T, peak acceleration about 5.77Δ/T². Acceleration is continuous, so motion is smoother.

3. Via points

To pass through intermediate points without stopping, plan a cubic for each segment and choose non-zero velocities at the via points (e.g. the average of the slopes of the adjoining segments, or zero where the slope changes sign) so that velocity is continuous.

Worked example

Problem. Move a joint from 10° to 70° in T = 3 s, starting and ending at rest, using a cubic.

θ″(0) = 2a2 = 40 °/s²; θ″(3) = 40 + 6(−4.444)(3) = −40 °/s².

With a quintic, peak velocity would be 1.875 × 60/3 = 37.5 °/s, but acceleration starts and ends at zero.

Key points to remember

  • Cubic: four conditions; quintic: six conditions.
  • Cubic peak velocity = 1.5Δ/T at mid-time.
  • Quintic gives continuous acceleration (less jerk) at the cost of higher peak velocity.
  • Via points need matched velocities.

Learning objectives

  • Explain incremental and absolute encoders and quadrature decoding.
  • Compute encoder resolution at the motor and at the joint, and speed from counts.
  • Explain accelerometers and gyroscopes in an IMU and estimate tilt with a complementary filter.

Recall before you start

Gear ratios; basic trigonometry.

1. Encoders

Incremental optical encoders have a slotted disc and produce two square waves A and B, 90° out of phase (quadrature), plus an index pulse Z once per revolution. Counting edges gives position; the phase order of A and B gives direction. With ×4 decoding (counting every edge of A and B), counts per revolution CPR = 4 × PPR.

Angular resolution = 360° / CPR Speed ω = 2π ΔN / (CPR Δt)

Absolute encoders output a unique code (often Gray code, which changes one bit at a time) for every position; an n-bit encoder resolves 360°/2n and keeps position after power loss. Multi-turn absolute encoders are standard on industrial robot joints.

2. Encoders on geared joints

An encoder on the motor shaft sees the joint motion multiplied by the gear ratio N, so joint resolution = motor resolution / N. Backlash in the gearbox, however, is not seen by a motor-side encoder.

3. IMUs

  • Accelerometer (MEMS): measures specific force, including gravity. At rest it gives tilt: pitch = atan2(ax, √(ay² + az²)). Noisy during motion.
  • Gyroscope: measures angular rate; integrating gives angle, which drifts with time because of bias.
  • Magnetometer: heading from Earth's field; disturbed by nearby steel and motors.

A complementary filter combines the short-term accuracy of the gyro with the long-term stability of the accelerometer:

θk = α(θk−1 + ω Δt) + (1 − α) θacc, α ≈ 0.98

Common MEMS IMU: MPU6050 (3-axis accelerometer + 3-axis gyro, I2C address 0x68).

Worked example

Problem. A 500 PPR encoder with ×4 decoding is on a motor driving a joint through a 50:1 gearbox. (a) Joint resolution? (b) 400 counts are recorded in 10 ms; what is the motor speed in rpm?

(a) CPR = 2000; motor resolution = 360/2000 = 0.18°; joint resolution = 0.18/50 = 0.0036°.

(b) Revolutions = 400/2000 = 0.2 rev in 0.01 s = 20 rev/s = 1200 rpm (joint: 24 rpm).

Key points to remember

  • Quadrature A/B gives direction; ×4 decoding gives CPR = 4 × PPR.
  • Absolute encoders remember position after power loss.
  • Gyro drifts; accelerometer is noisy; fuse them.
  • Motor-side encoders multiply resolution by N but do not see backlash.

Recall before you start

Ohm's law; similar triangles.

1. Strain-gauge force and torque sensors

A strain gauge changes resistance with strain: GF = (ΔR/R)/ε, with gauge factor GF ≈ 2 for metal foil gauges. Gauges are bonded to a flexure and connected in a Wheatstone bridge. For a quarter bridge with excitation Vex:

Vo ≈ Vex × GF × ε / 4

The millivolt output needs an amplifier (instrumentation amplifier or a load-cell ADC such as HX711). A 6-axis wrist F/T sensor measures three forces and three moments for assembly, polishing and collision detection.

2. Tactile and proximity sensors

  • Force-sensitive resistors (FSR) and tactile arrays on gripper fingers.
  • Inductive proximity (metal only), capacitive (most materials), photoelectric (through-beam, retro-reflective, diffuse), ultrasonic.
  • Limit switches and light curtains for safety.

3. Vision sensors

A camera maps a 3D point (X, Y, Z) in the camera frame to image coordinates by the pinhole model:

u = f X / Z, v = f Y / Z

Key parameters: resolution (pixels), frame rate, field of view, lens focal length, exposure and lighting. 2D cameras find position and orientation on a plane; 3D sensing uses stereo cameras, structured light or time-of-flight to obtain depth. Cameras must be calibrated (intrinsic and camera-to-robot extrinsic transform).

  • Compute overshoot, settling time and steady-state error.

Recall before you start

Laplace transform basics: derivative ↔ s, integral ↔ 1/s.

1. Open loop versus closed loop

In open-loop control the command is sent without measuring the result (a stepper motor driven by pulses, a timed conveyor). In closed-loop control a sensor measures the output, the error e = r − y is computed and the controller acts on the error (a servo joint with an encoder). Feedback rejects disturbances and parameter changes but can cause overshoot or instability if badly tuned.

2. Transfer functions and block diagrams

With zero initial conditions, the transfer function G(s) = Y(s)/U(s). Blocks in series multiply; for a forward path G and feedback H:

T(s) = Y(s)/R(s) = G(s) / (1 + G(s)H(s))

A DC motor's speed response to voltage is often approximated as first order: ω(s)/V(s) = K/(τs + 1).

3. Standard responses to a step input

  • First order K/(τs+1): reaches 63.2% in τ, about 98% in 4τ; no overshoot.
  • Second order ωn²/(s² + 2ζωns + ωn²): ζ < 1 underdamped (overshoot), ζ = 1 critically damped, ζ > 1 overdamped.
Mp = exp(−ζπ / √(1 − ζ²)), ts ≈ 4 / (ζωn) (2% criterion)

For a unity-feedback type-0 system with position constant Kp = G(0), the steady-state error to a unit step is ess = 1/(1 + Kp). An integrator in the loop (type 1) removes this error.

Worked example

Problem. A joint servo behaves as a second-order system with ζ = 0.5 and ωn = 4 rad/s. Find the percentage overshoot and settling time.

Mp = exp(−0.5π/0.866) = exp(−1.814) = 0.163 → 16.3%. ts ≈ 4/(0.5 × 4) = 2 s.

If the robot must not overshoot (e.g. when inserting a part), we would raise ζ towards 1.

Key points to remember

  • Feedback = measure, compare, correct.
  • T = G/(1 + GH).
  • ζ sets overshoot; ζωn sets settling time.
  • Integral action removes steady-state error to a step.

2. Ziegler–Nichols ultimate-gain method

  1. Set Ki = Kd = 0. Raise Kp until the output oscillates with constant amplitude.
  2. Record this ultimate gain Ku and the oscillation period Tu.
  3. Set Kp = 0.6Ku, Ti = 0.5Tu, Td = 0.125Tu; then Ki = Kp/Ti, Kd = KpTd.
  4. Fine-tune by hand: Z-N settings typically give about 25% overshoot.

3. Digital PID (sample time Ts)

uk = Kpek + KiTsΣej + Kd(ek − ek−1)/Ts
float Kp=4.8, Ki=19.2, Kd=0.3, Ts=0.01;   // gains from the example, 10 ms loop
float integ=0, prevErr=0;
float pid(float ref, float meas) {
  float e = ref - meas;
  integ += e * Ts;
  integ = constrain(integ, -50, 50);          // anti-windup clamp
  float deriv = (e - prevErr) / Ts;  prevErr = e;
  float u = Kp*e + Ki*integ + Kd*deriv;
  return constrain(u, -255, 255);             // actuator (PWM) limit
}
  • Run the loop at a fixed rate (timer interrupt or millis check).
  • Integral windup: when the actuator saturates, the integral keeps growing and causes big overshoot; clamp it.
  • Derivative amplifies noise; filter the measurement or the derivative term.

Worked example

Problem. In a Z-N test the DC motor speed loop oscillates steadily at Ku = 8 with period Tu = 0.5 s. Find PID gains.

Kp = 0.6 × 8 = 4.8; Ti = 0.25 s → Ki = 4.8/0.25 = 19.2; Td = 0.0625 s → Kd = 4.8 × 0.0625 = 0.30.

Key points to remember

  • P reacts to the present, I to the past, D to the predicted future error.
  • Z-N: Kp = 0.6Ku, Ti = Tu/2, Td = Tu/8.
  • Fixed sample time, anti-windup and derivative filtering are essential in code.
  • Tune on the real plant; models are approximate.

Recall before you start

1. What is a PLC?

A Programmable Logic Controller is a rugged industrial computer that reads sensors, executes a stored program and drives actuators. It replaced hard-wired relay panels. Parts: CPU with memory, power supply, digital and analog I/O modules (24 V DC inputs, relay or transistor outputs, 4–20 mA analog), communication (Ethernet/IP, PROFINET, Modbus TCP) and a programming device.

2. Scan cycle

  1. Read all inputs into the input image table.
  2. Execute the program rung by rung, top to bottom.
  3. Write the output image table to the outputs.
  4. Housekeeping and communication; repeat.

The scan time (typically 1–20 ms) limits how quickly the PLC can react; very short input pulses may be missed.

3. IEC 61131-3 languages

Ladder Diagram (LD), Function Block Diagram (FBD), Structured Text (ST), Instruction List (IL, deprecated) and Sequential Function Chart (SFC). OpenPLC supports these languages and runs on a PC, Raspberry Pi, Arduino or ESP32 boards.

import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
def dh(theta, d, a, alpha):                      # standard D-H link transform
    ct, st, ca, sa = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta), np.cos(alpha), np.sin(alpha)
    return np.array([[ct, -st*ca,  st*sa, a*ct],
                     [st,  ct*ca, -ct*sa, a*st],
                     [0,      sa,     ca,    d],
                     [0,       0,      0,    1]])
l1, l2 = 0.5, 0.3
def fk(q1, q2):
    T = dh(q1, 0, l1, 0) @ dh(q2, 0, l2, 0)
    return T[:2, 3]                               # x, y of the tool

Recall before you start

Velocity, angular velocity; frames (M2).

1. Wheeled mobile robots

Common drives: differential drive (two driven wheels + caster), car-like (Ackermann), omnidirectional (mecanum or omni wheels). For differential drive with wheel radius r, track width L and wheel speeds ωR, ωL:

v = r(ωR + ωL)/2, ω = r(ωR − ωL)/L, turning radius R = v/ω

Integrating v and ω over time gives the pose estimate (odometry), which drifts because of wheel slip.

2. AGVs and AMRs

AGVs (automated guided vehicles) follow fixed guides (magnetic tape, wires, QR markers) on predefined routes. AMRs (autonomous mobile robots) build or use a map with LiDAR and cameras and plan their own routes around obstacles.

3. Collaborative robots (cobots)

Cobots work alongside people without full fencing, under risk assessment per ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066. Collaborative modes:

  • Safety-rated monitored stop.
  • Hand guiding.
  • Speed and separation monitoring.
  • Power and force limiting.

4. SLAM

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping: the robot builds a map of an unknown environment while estimating its own pose in that map. Odometry predicts motion; LiDAR or camera observations of landmarks correct it; loop closure (recognising a revisited place) removes accumulated drift. Approaches include EKF-SLAM, particle-filter SLAM and graph-based SLAM (used by ROS2 slam_toolbox).

5. Robot vision

Vision lets robots detect and locate parts (template matching, feature detection, deep-learning detectors), inspect quality, read codes and guide motion (visual servoing). Vision results must be transformed into the robot frame using the calibrated camera transform (M2).

Worked example

Problem. A differential-drive robot has r = 0.05 m, L = 0.3 m, ωR = 10 rad/s, ωL = 8 rad/s. Find v, ω and R.

v = 0.05 × 18/2 = 0.45 m/s; ω = 0.05 × 2/0.3 = 0.333 rad/s (turning left); R = 0.45/0.333 = 1.35 m.

Key points to remember

  • Differential drive: v from the sum, ω from the difference of wheel speeds.
  • AGVs follow guides; AMRs navigate with maps.
  • Four collaborative modes; risk assessment is always required.
  • SLAM = map + localize together; loop closure removes drift.

Brief theory

Rx, Ry, Rz are 3×3 orthonormal matrices with det = 1 and R−1 = RT. An HTM T = [R p; 0 1] maps points from one frame to another; T−1 = [RT −RTp; 0 1]. Post-multiplication applies motions about the current frame; pre-multiplication about the fixed frame (L03, L04).

Aim

To implement a go-to-goal proportional controller for the Turtlesim robot in ROS2 and analyse path, error and convergence for different controller gains.

Learning objectives

  • Compute distance and heading errors from the robot pose and a goal.
  • Implement a proportional controller that publishes velocity commands until the goal is reached.
  • Log trajectories and analyse time to goal, path length and final error for different gains.

Equipment and software

  • Ubuntu with ROS2 and turtlesim (from P11)
  • Python 3 with Matplotlib for analysis

Brief theory

For pose (x, y, θ) and goal (xg, yg): distance error ρ = √((xg−x)² + (yg−y)²), heading error α = atan2(yg−y, xg−x) − θ, wrapped to [−π, π]. Control law: v = Kvρ (limited), ω = Khα; stop when ρ < tolerance. A large Kh turns quickly towards the goal; a large Kv relative to Kh produces wide curved paths or overshoot (L23, L24).

  1. Start turtlesim_node. Add go_to_goal.py to the turtle_lab package from P11, register it in setup.py and rebuild.
  2. Run ros2 run turtle_lab go_to_goal --ros-args -p xg:=8.0 -p yg:=8.0 and watch the turtle reach the goal.
  3. The node writes a CSV log (time, x, y). Plot the path for each run.
  4. Repeat for the gain pairs in Table 1, resetting the turtle each time (ros2 service call /reset std_srvs/srv/Empty).
  5. Compute time to goal, path length (sum of distances between logged points), straight-line distance and final error for each run.
  6. Write an analysis: which gains give the best trade-off between speed and path efficiency, and why?

# go_to_goal.py
import math, csv, time, rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from geometry_msgs.msg import Twist
from turtlesim.msg import Pose
class GoToGoal(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('go_to_goal')
        for n, v in [('xg', 8.0), ('yg', 8.0), ('kv', 1.0), ('kh', 4.0), ('tol', 0.05)]:
            self.declare_parameter(n, v)
        self.p = {n: self.get_parameter(n).value for n in ['xg', 'yg', 'kv', 'kh', 'tol']}
        self.pub = self.create_publisher(Twist, '/turtle1/cmd_vel', 10)
        self.create_subscription(Pose, '/turtle1/pose', self.cb, 10)
        self.t0 = time.time(); self.f = open(f"run_kv{self.p['kv']}_kh{self.p['kh']}.csv", 'w', newline='')
        self.w = csv.writer(self.f); self.w.writerow(['t', 'x', 'y'])
    def cb(self, pose):
        dx, dy = self.p['xg'] - pose.x, self.p['yg'] - pose.y
        rho = math.hypot(dx, dy)
        e = math.atan2(dy, dx) - pose.theta
        alpha = math.atan2(math.sin(e), math.cos(e))          # wrap to [-pi, pi]
        self.w.writerow([round(time.time() - self.t0, 3), pose.x, pose.y])
        cmd = Twist()
        if rho > self.p['tol']:
            cmd.linear.x = min(self.p['kv'] * rho, 2.0)
            cmd.angular.z = self.p['kh'] * alpha
        else:
            self.get_logger().info(f'Goal reached in {time.time() - self.t0:.2f} s, error {rho:.3f}')
            self.pub.publish(cmd); self.f.close(); raise SystemExit
        self.pub.publish(cmd)
def main():
    rclpy.init(); node = GoToGoal()
    try: rclpy.spin(node)
    except SystemExit: pass
    rclpy.shutdown()

Case 1.1 · Choosing a robot for a bag palletizing cell

Configurations, DOF matching, weighted decision matrix
Case 1.1: animated view

Context

A cement-additive plant fills 20 kg bags at 10 bags per minute and wants a robot to stack them on pallets, five bags per layer, eight layers. Bags are placed flat and sometimes turned 90° to interlock the layers.

Problem

Select the robot configuration and justify the choice so that a reviewer can challenge a single score instead of the whole decision.

Solution, step by step

  1. Count the task DOF. A flat bag needs x, y, z and one rotation about the vertical axis: four pose variables. A 6-axis robot would carry two axes the task never uses.
  2. Set criteria and weights from the brief. Payload fit 0.30 (20 kg plus gripper), reach 0.20 (a 1.2 × 1.0 m pallet plus infeed), speed 0.20 (6 s per bag), cost 0.15, footprint 0.15.
  3. ConfigurationPayloadReachSpeedCostFootprintWeighted score
    Cartesian gantry553323.85
    SCARA225553.50

Case 1.2 · Vacuum gripper for oily machined flanges

End effectors, gripping force, safety factor
Case 1.2: animated view

Context

A 1.5 kg machined flange, still wet with cutting oil, is lifted from its flat top face and moved sideways. The vacuum generator reaches 60 kPa below atmosphere. Cup friction on the oily face is μ ≈ 0.3. Peak vertical acceleration 5 m/s², peak horizontal 10 m/s², safety factor 2.

Problem

Choose the number and size of vacuum cups.

Solution, step by step

  1. Vertical lift. The cup must carry weight plus inertia with the safety factor: F = m(g + a)SF = 1.5(9.81 + 5)(2) = 44.4 N.
  2. Cup size for lift alone. A cup holds F = Δp·A. A = 44.4 / 60 000 = 7.4 × 10−4 m², so D = 30.7 mm. The next standard size is Ø40 mm, holding 75.4 N.
  3. Sideways motion. Friction must supply m·a = 1.5 × 10 = 15 N. With one Ø40 cup the friction available is μ(Fcup − mg) = 0.3(75.4 − 14.7) = 18.2 N: a safety factor of only 1.21.
  4. Fix. Two Ø40 cups give 150.8 N; friction available 0.3(150.8 − 14.7) = 40.8 N, a safety factor of 2.7. Two cups also resist tilting during acceleration.
What the good engineer did. For slippery parts, sideways acceleration, not weight, decides the gripper. Always check every direction of motion.
Common mistake. Sizing the cup for the part weight only, then watching parts slide off at full speed.

Case 1.3 · Repeatability is not accuracy: a vision-guided pick

Accuracy, repeatability, error budgets
Case 1.3: animated view

Context

A robot with repeatability ±0.02 mm must pick parts located by a camera and insert them into pockets with 1.0 mm total clearance. The robot's absolute accuracy (uncalibrated) is 0.8 mm; the camera's location error is 0.3 mm.

Problem

Will the cell work, and what should be improved first?

Solution, step by step

  1. Recognise the mode. The robot goes to coordinates computed from the camera, not to taught points, so accuracy, not repeatability, governs the error.
  2. Combine independent errors (root-sum-square). √(0.8² + 0.3²) = 0.85 mm against a 1.0 mm clearance: only 15% margin.
  3. Improve the largest term. Calibrating the robot brings accuracy to about 0.2 mm: √(0.2² + 0.3²) = 0.36 mm, a comfortable margin. Improving the camera first would only give √(0.8² + 0.1²) = 0.81 mm.
What the good engineer did. The ±0.02 mm figure on the datasheet hardly matters here; calibration of absolute accuracy does.
Common mistake. Choosing the robot by its repeatability for a task that never repeats a taught point.

Case 3.1 · Sizing the joint motors of a pick arm

Two-link dynamics, gravity and inertia torques, gearing
Case 3.1: animated view

Context

A two-link arm has point masses m1 = 3 kg and m2 = 2 kg (including payload) at the link ends, l1 = 0.45 m, l2 = 0.35 m. The worst case is the arm stretched horizontally while joint 1 accelerates at 4 rad/s². Gearboxes are 100:1 with 80% efficiency.

Problem

Find the peak joint torques and the motor torques.

Solution, step by step

  1. Gravity (static) torques at θ1 = θ2 = 0: τ1 = (m1 + m2)g l1 + m2g l2 = 22.07 + 6.87 = 28.94 N·m; τ2 = 6.87 N·m.
  2. Inertia terms at c2 = 1: M11 = (m1+m2)l1² + m2l2² + 2m2l1l2 = 1.888 kg·m²; M21 = m2l2² + m2l1l2 = 0.560 kg·m².
  3. Peak torques with θ1″ = 4 rad/s² (velocities zero at the start): τ1 = 28.94 + 1.888 × 4 = 36.49 N·m; τ2 = 6.87 + 0.560 × 4 = 9.11 N·m.
  4. Motor torques = τ/(Nη): joint 1 needs 0.456 N·m, joint 2 0.114 N·m. Choose motors with continuous ratings above these and peak ratings with margin for faster moves.
What the good engineer did. Gravity sets the floor; acceleration adds on top. Size on the worst pose and the worst acceleration together.
Common mistake. Sizing from the static torque only, then losing position when the arm accelerates outward.

Case 3.2 · Saving cycle time with the right profile

LSPB, cubic polynomial, velocity and acceleration limits
Case 3.2: animated view

Context

A pick-and-place joint swings 120° four times per cycle. The joint is limited to 180 °/s and 600 °/s².

Problem

Compare the fastest legal LSPB and cubic moves, and the effect on the cycle.

Solution, step by step

  1. LSPB. V²/a = 54° < 120°, so a cruise phase exists. T = Δ/V + V/a = 0.667 + 0.300 = 0.967 s.
  2. Cubic. Velocity limit: 1.5Δ/T ≤ 180 gives T ≥ 1.000 s. Acceleration limit: 6Δ/T² ≤ 600 gives T ≥ 1.095 s. So T = 1.095 s.
  3. Cycle effect. 4 × (1.095 − 0.967) = 0.51 s saved per cycle. On a 6 s cycle that is about 9% more parts per hour.
What the good engineer did. A cubic uses its acceleration limit only for an instant; LSPB holds the limits for longer and finishes sooner.
Common mistake. Picking the trajectory type out of habit without checking which limit is active.

Case 3.3 · Moving glass sheets without vibration

Cubic versus quintic, acceleration jumps
Case 3.3: animated view

Context

A robot moves fragile glass sheets with a 90° joint swing in 2 s. A cubic profile causes visible ringing at the start and end of every move. The joint velocity limit is 75 °/s.

Problem

Should the profile be changed, and what does it cost?

Solution, step by step

  1. Cubic. Acceleration jumps from 0 to 6Δ/T² = 135 °/s² instantly at t = 0, which excites vibration. Peak velocity 1.5Δ/T = 67.5 °/s.
  2. Quintic. Acceleration starts and ends at zero; peak ≈ 5.77Δ/T² = 129.9 °/s². But peak velocity is 1.875Δ/T = 84.4 °/s — above the 75 °/s limit.
  3. Fix. Stretch the quintic: T ≥ 1.875 × 90/75 = 2.25 s. The move takes 0.25 s longer but no longer rings, so the settling wait after each move disappears.
What the good engineer did. Smoothness costs a little peak velocity; check the velocity limit before switching to a quintic.
Common mistake. Switching to quintic at the same time T and silently exceeding the joint's velocity limit.

Case 4.1 · Choosing an encoder for a ±0.05 mm placement

Encoder resolution, gearing, error budgets
Case 4.1: animated view

Context

A robot joint at 0.6 m from the tool must support ±0.05 mm placement. The motor drives the joint through a 100:1 gearbox. Good practice: encoder resolution about one tenth of the accuracy target.

Problem

What encoder resolution is needed?

Solution, step by step

  1. Tool resolution target = 0.05/10 = 0.005 mm at a radius of 600 mm: Δθjoint = 0.005/600 = 8.33 × 10−6 rad = 0.000477°.
  2. At the motor the gearbox multiplies the angle by 100: 0.0477° per count, so CPR ≥ 360/0.0477 = 7540.
  3. Choose. With ×4 decoding, PPR ≥ 1885: a 2500 PPR encoder (10 000 CPR) or a 17-bit absolute encoder (131 072 counts) both qualify; the absolute one also avoids homing.
What the good engineer did. Gearing helps resolution, but backlash after the encoder is invisible to it; the mechanical budget still matters.
Common mistake. Choosing an encoder equal to the accuracy target, leaving nothing for backlash and servo error.

Case 4.2 · A load cell for a force-limited gripper

Strain gauges, bridges, amplification, ADC resolution
Case 4.2: animated view

Context

A gripper finger carries a full-bridge strain-gauge flexure (GF = 2, 5 V excitation) that sees 800 µε at its 50 N rating. The signal goes to a 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC.

Problem

Design the amplification and find the force resolution.

Solution, step by step

  1. Bridge output at full load: Vo ≈ Vex·GF·ε = 5 × 2 × 800 × 10−6 = 8 mV.
  2. Gain to use the ADC range: 3.3/0.008 = 412. Choose 400 for headroom: full load gives 3.2 V.
  3. Resolution. One count is 3.3/4096 = 0.806 mV; full load spans 3971 counts, so 50/3971 = 0.0126 N per count.
What the good engineer did. Bridge signals are millivolts; amplification is what turns them into useful ADC counts.
Common mistake. Feeding an 8 mV signal straight into the ADC: the whole load range would be ten counts.

Case 4.3 · Pneumatic or electric for a transfer axis?

Actuator selection, cylinder force
Case 4.3: animated view

Context

A 200 mm transfer moves 5 kg parts at 30 cycles per minute. A 25 mm bore cylinder at 0.6 MPa is proposed. Later, the product mix changes and the axis must stop at five positions.

Problem

Is the cylinder adequate now, and what changes later?

Solution, step by step

  1. Force. F = pA = 0.6 × 10⁶ × π(0.025)²/4 = 294.5 N, far above the friction and acceleration loads of a 5 kg part.
  2. Air use. Each stroke uses about 0.68 L of free air (cylinder volume × pressure ratio). Two strokes per cycle at 30 cycles/min ≈ 41 L/min — cheap for a plant compressor.
  3. Five positions. Air is compressible, so a plain cylinder stops reliably only at its end stops. Five positions need a servo-electric axis (or servo-pneumatics).
What the good engineer did. Pneumatics win for two-position, fast, cheap moves; positioning flexibility needs an electric servo.
Common mistake. Adding mid-stroke stops to a pneumatic cylinder and fighting overshoot forever.

Case 5.1 · Tuning a conveyor-tracking motor

Ziegler–Nichols tuning, overshoot, retuning
Case 5.1: animated view

Context

A conveyor drive behaves as G(s) = 1/((0.2s + 1)(0.05s + 1)(0.02s + 1)). An ultimate-gain test gives Ku = 19.25 and Tu = 0.171 s. The robot that tracks the belt tolerates at most 15% overshoot.

Problem

Tune a PID controller and check it against the overshoot limit.

Solution, step by step

  2. Simulate. The step response overshoots by about 64%: Z-N aims for quick disturbance rejection, not low overshoot.

What the good engineer did. Z-N gives a starting point; final gains come from the requirement and the measured response.

Case 5.2 · A sorting line that missed parts

PLC timers, counters, tracking
Case 5.2: animated view

Context

Caps travel at 0.25 m/s; a metal sensor sits 0.5 m before a diverter. The PLC starts a 2 s timer on detection and fires the diverter when it ends. Production rises from 20 to 60 caps per minute and metal caps begin to reach the wrong bin.

Problem

Explain the failures and redesign the logic.

Solution, step by step

  1. Spacing. At 60 caps/min and 0.25 m/s, caps are 0.25 m apart, so two caps are always between the sensor and the diverter.
  2. Why it fails. A single timer can track only one cap. When a second metal cap arrives before the first is diverted, the timer is busy and the second cap is lost.
  3. Redesign. Track caps with a belt encoder and a shift register: one bit per 50 mm of belt, 10 bits from sensor to diverter. Detection writes a 1 into the first bit; every 50 mm the register shifts; the diverter fires when the last bit is 1. Any number of caps can be tracked.
What the good engineer did. Timers track one event; shift registers track a queue of events on a moving belt.
Common mistake. Testing the sorter at low rate only, where a timer happens to work.

Case 5.3 · PLC–robot handshake in machine tending

Case 5.3: animated view

Context

A robot loads a CNC lathe from a conveyor. The lathe cycle is 45 s; a load-and-unload with one gripper takes 12 s. The PLC coordinates the conveyor, the robot and the lathe door.

Problem

Define the handshake, and estimate the gain from a double gripper (7 s swap).

  1. StepSignalFrom → To

    2MACHINE_DONE, DOOR_OPENLathe → PLC

Case 6.1 · AGV or AMR for hospital pharmacy runs

Mobile robots, AGV versus AMR
Case 6.1: animated view

Context

A hospital wants a robot to carry medicines from the pharmacy to six wards along 400 m corridors shared with staff, patients and trolleys. Ward layouts change every few months.

Problem

Analyze whether an AGV or an AMR suits the hospital.

Solution, step by step

  1. Infrastructure. An AGV needs tape or markers along every route, and re-laying them each time a ward moves; an AMR uses its map and re-plans.
  2. Obstacles. An AGV stops and waits when a trolley blocks the tape; an AMR drives around it. In busy corridors, waiting dominates the AGV's trip time even though its top speed (1.0 m/s) is higher than the AMR's average (0.8 m/s).
  3. Trip time. Unblocked: AGV 400 s, AMR 500 s. With five 60 s blockages per trip, the AGV takes 700 s while the AMR, detouring in about 10 s each, takes 550 s.
  4. Conclusion. An AMR. AGVs suit fixed routes with little traffic, such as a factory line.
What the good engineer did. Analyze the environment, not just the top speed: variability decides between AGV and AMR.
Common mistake. Comparing robots on their brochure speed instead of the trip time in the real environment.

Case 6.2 · Why the digital twin disagreed with RoboAnalyzer

Model validation, point mass versus distributed mass
Case 6.2: animated view

Context

A student's Python twin of a 2R arm gave tool positions that were wrong at every pose; after a fix they matched RoboAnalyzer exactly. Gravity torques then differed by up to 2× for one link.

Problem

Analyze the causes of both discrepancies.

Solution, step by step

  1. FK wrong everywhere, fixed by one change. The usual cause is units: angles entered in degrees into NumPy's radian functions. cos(30) in radians is 0.154, not 0.866.
  2. Torques differ. The twin modelled each link as a point mass at its end; RoboAnalyzer uses distributed link mass. For a 2 kg, 0.5 m link held horizontally: point mass gives m g l = 9.81 N·m, a uniform rod gives m g l/2 = 4.91 N·m — a factor of 2.
  3. Make the comparison valid. Use the same mass model in both (or enter point-mass-like inertias in RoboAnalyzer), then compare.
What the good engineer did. When two models disagree, list their assumptions side by side before trusting either.
Common mistake. Tuning the twin's numbers until they match, without finding the modelling difference.

Case 6.3 · Speed and separation monitoring for a cobot

Collaborative robots, separation distance
Case 6.3: animated view

Context

A cobot drives screws at 0.5 m/s next to an operator. A safety scanner detects people with 0.1 s reaction time; the robot stops in 0.3 s and travels 75 mm while stopping. Walking speed is taken as 1.6 m/s; a 100 mm margin is added.

Problem

Find the minimum protective separation distance (simplified form).

Solution, step by step

  1. Human travel during reaction and stop: 1.6 × (0.1 + 0.3) = 0.64 m.
  2. Robot travel during reaction: 0.5 × 0.1 = 0.05 m; plus stopping distance 0.075 m.
  3. Total S = 0.64 + 0.05 + 0.075 + 0.10 = 0.865 m. Inside this distance the robot must already be stopping; slowing the robot near people shrinks S.
What the good engineer did. Separation distance is mostly the human's travel during the robot's stopping time; faster stopping buys space.
Common mistake. Using only the robot's stopping distance and forgetting how far the person walks meanwhile.
